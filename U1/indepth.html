Unit 1: One-Variable Data

BASIC DEFINITIONS

Statistics fundamentally deals with analysis of data, and in data, the individuals are the objects that are described, while a variable is a characteristic of an individual.

The population covers all the individuals, while a sample only covers some. Since it is often impractical to collect data on every single individual, samples are often used instead for data analysis and making some inference about the population.

The two types of variables to know are quantitative and categorical (also called qualitative).

> Quantitative variables represent numerical measurements (they can be quantified), like shoe sizes, GPA, or the number of pets you have.

> Categorical variables place individuals into one of several groups (or categories), think blood type, favorite soda, or native language.

Usually, the variable type is quite obvious. One thing to note, however, is that when numbers are involved, the variable is often quantitative but not always. For example, if the variable is the first 3 digits of a phone number (area code), it’s categorical, even though it’s a literal number. It wasn’t measured numerically, but grouped into categories (301, 201, etc).

If you’re ever uncertain, ask yourself, “Does it make sense to take and find an average of these variables?” If so, the variable is quantitative, otherwise, it’s categorical.

CATEGORICAL DISPLAYS

Now we will discuss how data is visually presented. You are likely already familiar with many displays of data, but at least skim this part of the unit anyway.

Categorical data often appear as frequency tables, bar charts, and pie charts, which are all pretty straightforward to interpret.

> Two-way frequency tables that organize two categorical variables by a row variable and a column variable.

Though useful, frequency tables can be dense and harder to interpret. Especially if you’re displaying data on the news or something, most people are only looking for a quick visualization. 

Segmented bar graphs

Segmented bar graphs are good at comparing the distributions of two datasets. You can visually see the composition of each variable.

Mosaic graphs 

Mosaic graphs are essentially 2d segmented bar graphs, where the width of the bars changes according to its relative frequency of the x variable.

Bar charts, like any format of data representation, need labels. Here, the x-axis represents the year, and the y-axis represents the number of police officers. Note that the bars in bar charts are all equally wide so that the size of a bar chart corresponds linearly with the size of its y-value, making for an accurate representation of data. Also note that the bars are separated by a space between each bar.

Good practice for formatting involves having appropriate labels, scales, and equally wide bars. making the bars equally wide

QUANTITIATIVE DISPLAYS

But for the vast majority of this course, we will work with quantitative data. Common displays are dotplots, stem-and-leaf plots, histograms, and box-and-whiskers plots (boxplots).

Dot plots 

Dot plots represent data so that you can easily see the shape of the distribution. Each dot takes the place of one value.

Stem-and-leaf plots 

Stem-and-leaf plots are also a way to represent the data. You can easily see the shape of the distribution, except this time the axis is up-and-down instead of left-to-right. The stem usually represents the tens digit of the number, while the leaf represents the ones digit, as shown in the key. For example, a stem of 0 and a leaf of 5 corresponds to the value 05.

Histograms 

Histograms measure the frequency distribution of quantitative variables. Note that these differ from box plots in that they can only measure frequency, they use quantitative variables, and the bins don’t have a gap between them. The bins measure how many values of the distribution fall within that interval the bin spans.

Boxplots

The box plot is a graphical representation of the five-number summary, which is the [min, Q1, median, Q3, max] of the dataset.

Tukey’s Outliers are values of the dataset outside the range [Q1 - 1.5 * IQR, Q3 + 1.5 * IQR]. Q1 and Q3 are the 25th and 75th percentiles, respectively.

MORE DEFINITIONS

> The mean is the average value of all data points in the dataset. Because it depends on all values and it is non-resistant to outliers. 

> The median can be defined as the 50th percentile of the dataset, meaning 50 percent of all students are at or below the median, and 50 percent of all students are at or above the median. If you’re doing a median by hand, you should write all of the data in ascending order, for example 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 9. Then, simply cross out the smallest and largest values and repeat until you get to the center, which is 4, 6 in this case. If you only have one number remaining, that’s the median. If you have two, take the average of them and that’s the median. Because the median ignores the extreme values, it is resistant to outliers. 

> The mode will not be used much in this course, but it is the value of your variable with the highest frequency.

DESCRIBING DISTRIBUTIONS

When you are asked to describe a distribution, specifically note the shape, outliers, the center, and the spread (aka variability) (SOCS), and do it all IN CONTEXT.

Shape

Some things to mention are symmetry, modality/peaks, uniformity, gaps, and especially skewedness. The phrase “skewed right” or “skewed left” is quite misleading for many people: the direction of skewness is towards the long tail, NOT where most observations are clustered.

Outliers

Mention points that deviate from the overall pattern/structure of the distribution, but make sure to say that they appear to be outliers; you’re not proving they are here, you’re just noting their strange existence. If there appear to be no outliers, address that too.

Center

You can use the mean, median, or (rarely, if ever) mode. You don’t need to justify why you chose that particular center, but make a habit of selecting the best option. If the distribution is skewed, use median. If the distribution looks roughly symmetric and normal, use mean.

Spread

Common measures of spread include the range, IQR, or standard deviation (which we will discuss in the following section).

VARIANCE AND STANDARD DEVIATION

You might notice that the measures of variability we have talked about so far only need a few data scores to calculate and ignore much of the data. In reality, measures that take every data point into account are much more helpful.

Let’s say you are measuring counting the number of petals on some flowers and your dataset of petals per flower turns out to be

        2, 5, 7, 8, 8

The deviation of data point xi is xi - x̄ (basically, how far it strays from the mean). So the deviation of the 5-petaled flower is

        5 - 6 = -1 petals

This is just for one flower, though. We want to somehow standardize the deviations of all the points in the dataset and have a single, concise bit of info on variability. Could we just take the average of all the deviations? Kind of a terrible idea. Notice the variability of our 5-petaled flower was negative, that’s because it was less than the mean. But of course, the mean is not the mean without some data points being greater than it, other deviations will be positive and start canceling each other out. In fact, the sum of the deviations (and by extension, the average) will always be 0…

        n = # of data points

        X = sum of all datapoints; ∑xi

        ∑ (xi - x̄) / n = (X - n * x̄) / n = x̄ - x̄ = 0.

0 variability implies the data is completely uniform, which is clearly not the case.

If the issue was that the deviations were different signs and canceling each other out, should we just take the absolute value of each deviation and then take the average? Also no, though you’d be forgiven for considering it. For small datasets we could, but think about how impractical it would become the larger they get. Furthermore, when we alter the data in our calculations, we don’t just affect the numerical value, but also the units. Cubing 1 meter doesn’t give you 1m, it gives you 1m3. We can cube root to get a meter back, but what about absolute value? How do you turn a |petal| back into a petal??

We need a method to average the magnitude of deviations, and without irreversibly screwing with the units. What we do is square each deviation and take the average, giving us the variance, and then take the square root for the standard deviation.

In a population of size N…

        variance (σ2) = (1/N) * ∑ (xi - x̄)2

        std. dev. (σ) = sqrt( (1/N) * ∑(xi - x̄)2 )

Except…the formulas change slightly when the dataset is a sample rather than a population.

(Note: letter variables for samples are Latin, while they are Greek for population)

In a sample of size n…

        variance (s^2) = (1/(n-1)) * ∑ (xi - x̄)2

        Std. dev (s) = sqrt( (1/(n-1)) * ∑ (xi - x̄)2 )

Basically the only thing that changes you’re dividing by 1 less the number of individuals that make up the dataset.

> Why? Bessel’s correction. The basic idea is this: when you randomly select a sample from a population, you are likely to get values that are near the average and far from the outliers, because y’know, they’re outliers. As a result, xi - x-bar tends to underestimate xi - mu. To compensate, we divide by 1/(n-1) instead of 1/n.

> See Bessel's correction - Wikipedia 

DENSITY CURVES

A density curve describes a distribution with the area under the curve between two values being equal to the proportion of all observations that fall within the range. Naturally, this would imply that the curve is always on or above the x-axis; negative proportions wouldn’t make sense. Furthermore, the area under the entire curve is 1, since it covers the entire range, 100% of the observations would be within it.

The equals-area point is the median, since it divides the area of the curve in half, and the balancing point is the mean (denoted as μ). Imagine the curve had a uniform mass; it would balance at the mean. A symmetric density curve would then see its mean and median be equal.

A Normal distribution is a special density curve that is symmetric and unimodal (bell-shaped). Consequently, the mean and median are the same. A normal distribution’s mean and standard deviation are denoted in the form of N(μ, σ).

The Empirical Rule states that, in a Normal distribution, 68% of the values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean, 95% of the values fall within 2 standard deviations, and 99.7% of the values fall within 3 standard deviations.

> This rule is also called the 68-95-99.7 Rule.

STANDARD NORMAL DISTRIBUTION

Of all the normal distributions the Standard Normal Distribution is the one you will encounter the most throughout the entire course. It has a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1; N(0,1). This is helpful because we can compare values from different distributions using z-scores.

A z-score is the number of standard deviations a value is from the mean

        z = (x - μ) / σ

You’ll see z-scores come up all the time :)
