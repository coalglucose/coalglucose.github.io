Unit 5A: Sampling Distributions and Confidence Intervals

INTRO TO SAMPLING DISTRIBUTIONS

Up until this point, we have been making inferences about parameters (from the population) with statistics (from a sample). Now, the foundational concept for the rest of the course will be sampling distributions. Rather than focusing on one sample, a sampling distribution is formed from all statistics of the same size, from the same population.

To clarify, let’s use an example. Let’s say you want to know the average number of pets per household there are in a certain country. Previously, we would take a sample of households from the country, let’s say of sample size 100, and calculate the average number of pets. In a sampling distribution, we would now take all possible samples of size 100 from the population, take all of those sample means, and use them to form a distribution.

Though, it’s incredibly impractical to actually take all of those samples. Instead, we can simulate the distribution.

There are 3 key assumptions we must satisfy before using a sampling distribution.

  1. Randomness. If our statistic is an unbiased estimator, the mean of the distribution would be equal to the statistic.

> Either the sample is stated to be randomly obtained, or you state that you must assume it is.

  1. Normality (but only approximately). The sampling distribution can never be completely normal as the normal distribution covers the domain of all real numbers. But if the sampling distribution is roughly normal, we can still approximate probabilities for sampling distributions.

> Determining if a sampling distribution is approximately normally distributed depends on if you’re working with sample proportions or sample means, we’ll get into specifics later.

  1. Independence. The samples need independence so we may calculate the standard deviations (recall U4). Also note that the standard deviation of a sampling distribution is called the standard error.

> Just like in U4, establish this with the 10% Rule

There are two big differences between the sampling distribution of sample means and sample proportions, the first being how we establish (approximate) normality.

For sample proportions, we use the Success/Failure Condition.

For sample means, there are 3 ways…

  1. Apply the Central Limit Theorem (CLT). If the sample size is at least 30, we may assume the sampling distribution of sample means is approximately normally distributed, regardless of the shape of the distribution of the parameter.
  2. A Normal Probability Plot (NPP) graphs the data in such a way that if the data are approximately linear, the distribution is approximately normally distributed.
  3. If the population is given to be approximately normally distributed, we can assume that the sampling distribution of sample means is too

The other difference has to do with the standard error.

> For proportions, SE = pq / sqrt(n)  

> For sample means, SE = σ / sqrt(n)

But for means, you will often not be given the standard deviation of the population. Because you would need to know the mean for that, and what’s the point of estimating a parameter you already know? When σ is unknown,

> SE = s / sqrt(n)

Calculating the z-score is the same as ever, only the standard deviation is now for the sampling distribution, making it the standard error.

CONFIDENCE INTERVALS

A point estimate is a statistic that estimates the parameter. A confidence interval on the other hand, is an interval of plausible values for the parameter. Each interval has a confidence level, and a very important distinction to stress it that the confidence applies to the method used to construct the interval, not the confidence in a single interval. So for example, with 90% confidence, if we generated many intervals (remember: probability applies long-term), 90% of them would capture the true parameter.

A CI is constructed by

        estimate ± ME = estimate ± crit. value * SE

> Note that ME is shorthand for the margin of error

Recall how to transform data to z- and t-scores: the confidence level (C%) is the area under the Normal or t-distribution, and the boundaries are the critical values.

For z-critical values, it’s just a matter of using invNorm as we have this entire course. t-critical values are obtained from a chart and also take degrees of freedom (d.f) into account.

> d.f = n - 1

> If the exact d.f is not listed on the chart, apply the Conservative Rule and use the closest value that is less than the calculated d.f.
