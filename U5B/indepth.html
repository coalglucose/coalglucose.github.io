Unit 5B: Hypothesis Testing

HYPOTHESES

Recall that the purpose of a confidence interval is to estimate an unknown population parameter with sample data. In hypothesis testing, we have two competing claims (hypotheses) about a parameter.

The two hypotheses we have are…

> The null hypothesis (H0) is the claim we initially assume to be true; evidence is weighed against this. H0 is also called the claim of “no difference,” as we assume that the population parameter is equal to the hypothesized value from the sample. 

> The alternative hypothesis (HA or H1) is the claim that could be true and what we’re finding evidence for. HA will naturally be some form of inequality: >hyp. value, <hyp. value, or ≠hyp.value.

Consider this example…

> A chemist invents an additive to increase the life of an automobile battery. Without the additive, the mean battery life is 36 months.

> Let μ = mean battery life with the additive (in months)

> H0: μ = 36        HA: μ

But we can never definitively prove a hypothesis. We’re taking data from a sample, so there are just too many confounding variables to be completely certain. Therefore, we never “accept”  a hypothesis. We either reject or fail to reject H0 based on our evidence from the sample. Since we initially assume H0 to be true, only strong evidence from the sample that contradicts H0 will lead us to believe HA.

How strong would our evidence need to be, exactly? It depends. Just like with confidence levels from U5A.

DECISION

If the test value falls within a certain range of values known as the critical (rejection) region, that will indicate a significant difference to reject H0. Otherwise, the test value will be in the non-critical (non-rejection) region, indicating any difference was likely due insignificant and due to chance, so H0 should not be rejected.

The critical value (c.v) is what separates the regions. Our good old friend the Normal distribution and our good new friend the t-distribution will help us out. Unless you hate them. In which case, I’m sorry you’re taking a statistics class.

Remember how the area under the distributions correspond to probability? The area of the critical region is equal to our critical value, ɑ.

> A telephone company representative estimates that fewer than 40% of its customers have a landline. To test this hypothesis, the representative selected 100 customers and found that 37% of them have a landline. At ɑ=0.01, is there enough evidence to support the representative’s claim?

> Let p = true proportion of customers who have a landline

Now, we state the type of test we’re conducting, the hypotheses and relevant stats.

Since we’re working with proportions and just 1 sample (we’ll work with multiple in U5C), our critical value will be a z-value, the type of test is a 1-proporition Z-test. The names are pretty self-explanatory.

> 1-proportion Z-test

> H0: p = 0.4        H1: p < 0.4

> ɑ = 0.01

We calculate the z-score and, as in 5A, we get zcv from invNorm. Don’t forget to draw a picture and state whether or not H0 is rejected.

0.2709 is our p-value, the probability of obtaining a result as or more extreme than the sample given H0 is true. Now, we write our concluding paragraph

> Since the p-value > ɑ (0.2709 > 0.01), the representative must fail to reject H0 at the 0.01 level of significance. The sample provides enough evidence to support the claim that 40% of the company’s customers have a landline.

ERRORS

Obviously, there will be times were our conclusion is incorrect.

Type I Error: Rejecting a true H0. This can also be thought of as a false positive, since we incorrectly believe we our evidence supporting HA is strong enough.

        P(Type I Error) = ɑ (the level of significance)

Type II Error: Failing to reject a false H0. Similarly, this is a false negative as we don’t think there’s enough evidence to support HA.

        P(Type II Error) = ꞵ

Ideally, the probability of avoiding both errors would be 0%. But there is a trade-off: as alpha increases, beta decreases. Depending on the context, a Type I may be more dangerous than a Type II, or vice versa, so different values of alpha are used.

The power of a test = 1 - ꞵ. Conceptually, it is the probability of correctly rejecting a false H0.

Power would increase as beta decreases, but we have no direct control over beta. Instead, we increase the power of a test by either decreasing alpha (which makes it less likely to reject H0) or by increasing our sample size (because we would have a more accurate read on the true value).
