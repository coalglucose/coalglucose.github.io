Unit 3: Collecting Data 

BIAS

So far, we have been analyzing data. Now, we will focus how we can collect that data. But in practice, there is no perfect method to collect data because of bias. In statistics, bias refers to a systematic tendency in gathering data that produces an inaccurate (biased) depiction of reality.

The first type of bias we will look at is selection bias. As the name suggests, these biases arise from the way a sample is selected.

1. Undercoverage

Sometimes, a sample does a fundamentally poor job of representing the population of interest.

> Imagine you want to know the percentage Blair juniors are taking Advanced Geometry (I loved that class). But if you only ask Magnet juniors, there will be severe undercoverage; most Blair juniors are not in the Magnet, and their answers are not being recorded.

2. Convenience Sampling

Here, data is collected from an easily accessible sample, with individuals that may not be representative of the population of interest. Essentially: don’t be lazy with how you take your sample.

> If you want to know how many people in your grade want to major in chemistry, asking in the middle of orgo is not a great idea.

3. Voluntary Response

Similar to convenience sampling, these data are from individuals voluntarily offering their responses (get it?). Why is this an issue? People with strong opinions are more likely to bother going through the process of sharing their views.

> Let’s say a company creates a product and asks customers to call in to give their reviews. Unless you really love or really hate it, you’re not likely to take the time to make a phone call and share your thoughts with the employee on the other end. Thus, the data tends to be disproportionately affected by extreme answers.

Next, there are response biases, which come from the respondents in a sample.

1. Wording of the Question

The tone of a question should be as neutral and unambiguous as possible to avoid swaying respondents’ answers. For example…

> Asking “Given that hundreds of thousands of innocent, sentient animals are subjected to terrifying and painful experiments in the name of corporate profit, are do you favor or oppose the practice of animal testing?” will frame animal testing from a completely negative point of view.

2. Order of Choices

When respondents may be bored/tired/disengaged, they may stop processing information deeply. Instead of weighing every option carefully, they may latch onto and select the fisrst reasonable choice they see to save their time and effort. In these cases, the order of the choices can be significant. The choice presented first can be disproportionately selected, and this may be influential in a scenario such as this:

> When a person who doesn’t care much about politics goes into the ballot box to vote and sees candidates that they haven’t heard of, they will often simply vote for the first name listed under the office.  

3. Respondent Ignorance

Sometimes people just don’t know ¯\_(ツ)_/¯ 

> When a kid takes a survey about if they support or reject the idea of installing a particle accelerator in their community, they’ll probably arbitrarily select an option. Unless if they’re young Sheldon, they likely won’t know what it is.

4. Respondent Lies

Sometimes people just lie :( This often occurs when asking more taboo questions.

> “Have you cheated on an exam?” If a student has, they probably won’t share. (Don’t cheat guys, seriously.)

5. Appearance/Behavior of Interviewer

Justified or not, the interviewer could influence a respondent’s answer.

> Male and female interviewers ask random groups of students, “Would you vote for a female president?”. Male students are significantly more likely to say “Yes” if their interviewer is female versus male.

Finally, there is non-response bias. In a sense, this could also fall into response bias as it concerns respondents just…not responding. Be careful not to mix this up with undercoverage, though. Non-response = you include them in the sample, but they don’t respond, while undercoverage = you don’t include them in the sample at all.

> Let’s say you’re conducting a survey by calling adults from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m to see what they think about the new tax cuts. The bias is that people who work traditional 9-5 jobs are less likely to answer the phone or participate than those who don’t, so the survey responses will skew heavily towards populations such as retirees, stay at home parents, or unemployed people.  

When you’re asked to identify the type of bias, name the category of bias (selection, respondent, non-response), why the bias is present, how it affects the sample, and (if applicable), the specific type of bias (voluntary response, respondent lies, etc.).

SAMPLING METHODS

Since we wish to be good statisticians, we need sampling methods that minimize bias heheh.

A simple random sample (SRS) is a sample where every individual had the same probability of being selected. Basically, any sample that’s randomly selected with nothing fancy going on. There are two main ways to take an SRS in this course, one of which is kind of goofy and the other is pretty disgusting.

> Names in a Hat: Write down the names (or some other identifier) of all the individuals on identical slips of paper (so that people can’t feel around for differently shaped papers). Place the slips in a hat, shake vigorously (so the various slips corresponding to different treatments are thoroughly mixed), and select however many names you need without replacement.

> Random Digits Table:

Give each member of the population a numerical label with the same number of digits. Use as few digits as possible. Then, read as many digits as you need off the table. The paragraph you need to write for an RDT rather long-winded, though.

“Read consecutive groups of digits of the appropriate length from left to right across a line in Table D. Ignore any group of digits that wasn’t used as a label or that duplicates a label already in the sample. Stop when you have chosen n different labels.”

Stratified sampling involves taking a heterogeneous population and splitting the members into homogenous subgroups (strata), then taking an SRS of individuals from each stratum. You are taking some individuals from all of the subgroups. This is often used when populations have very diverse subgroups and helps studying differences between the subgroups.

> For instance, say we’re studying the reading comprehension abilityies of kids aged 4 - 10. Kids aged 10 will be able to read at a signfidixantly higher level than four year olds, so we can stratify into subgroups (ages 4-6, 7-8, 9-10) to get more specific results for each subgroup.

Cluster sampling is sort of an inverted stratified sample. From a homogeneous population, heterogeneous subgroups (clusters) are formed, and using an SRS, some of the clusters are chosen. In other words, you are taking all the individuals from some of the subgroups.

> If you continue to subdivide clusters into more targeted groups, it becomes multi-stage sampling. 

Lastly, know systematic sampling, where every nth individual of the population is chosen to be in the sample.

> With a list of all the student names, select every 5th student.

EXPERIMENTS

So far, we’ve been conducting observational studies, where we take samples to determine what’s already there. But often, we want to impose a certain change before observing responses. This is an experiment, and the change itself is the treatment.

New medicine is a very common example of an experiment. A well-designed experiement needs to follow the 4 Principles of Universal Experiment Design:

  1. Randomization

Exactly what it sounds like. Assign the treatments randomly.

> For 100 patients in the experiment, place all names in a hat, written on identical slips of paper. Then, shake the hat vigorously and select 50 names without replacement. These 50 will receive the new medicine and the remaining 50 will receive a placebo.

  1. Control

NOT exactly what it sound like. This does NOT refer to having a control group, it refers to having control for confounding variables.

> We can control for outside factors such as making sure all experimental units take the pill at the same time of day and have similar diets.

  1. Comparison 

Again, exactly what it sounds like. Have different treatments so you can compare results.

> You need to give the new medicine to a group of people, but if you have no baseline to compare it to, your results are useless. You need to have a control group to compare it against, at the bare minimum.

  1. Replication

Basically, your sample needs to be large enough to ensure that your results are statistically significant, not just up to chance.

Just like with sampling for observational studies, there are different ways to design your experiment. The types of experimental design you should be familiar with are as follows…

Completely randomized. Each individual is assigned a treatment randomly, no funny business.

Randomized block design. Some funny business. This is similar to stratified sampling, where the population of individuals are first split into subgroups (blocks). From there, randomly assign the treatments within the blocks. Or…

Matched pairs design. Even more funny business. These experiments have two treatments and the subjects can be grouped in to pairs, either because they have some blocking variable or because a subject can be its own control.
